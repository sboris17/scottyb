# Why Everyone Started A Podcast

**Topic:** The podcast pivot, and why it happened everywhere at once
**Angle:** It isn't a trend. It's the correct answer to four separate structural problems — which is exactly why everybody found it simultaneously, and exactly why it stopped working.
**Audience:** Anyone who has watched three creators they follow announce a podcast in the same month.
**Tone:** Amused, then serious. The joke opens it; the ceiling closes it.
**Open on:** Everyone bought the same two chairs.
**Close on:** A crowded room with better chairs.

---

## Script

At some point in the last few years, every creator you follow bought the same two microphones, the same two chairs, and the same slightly-too-dark room.

It happened so uniformly that it became a joke. New podcast announcement, same set, same boom arms, same table. And the standard read is that it's a trend — everybody copying everybody, the way everybody suddenly made the same kind of video in every previous era.

That's not what happened.

What actually happened is that four different people were sitting with four completely different problems, and all four problems have the same solution. So they didn't copy each other. They arrived, independently, in the same room.

And working out which four problems tells you a lot about the state of the industry — including why this move stopped working almost immediately after everyone made it.

Problem one is money, and it's the least interesting but the most decisive.

Edited long-form content gets more expensive every single year. The audience adjusts to whatever you did last time, so last year's production is this year's floor, and holding position costs more each cycle. Bigger sets, longer edits, more people. And the ad rate doesn't care — nobody pays you more per view because the video cost more to make.

A podcast breaks that. Two chairs, two microphones, two hours. The cost per minute is close to nothing and, crucially, it does not escalate — because nobody expects a podcast to look more impressive than it did last year. It's the only format in modern online video with a flat cost curve.

For a creator watching their margins get eaten by their own production standards, that is not a stylistic choice. That's an escape.

Problem two is ideas.

The hardest part of running any channel isn't filming or editing. It's the question that arrives every single week, forever: what am I making this time. Formats burn out. Concepts run dry. A creator four years in has usually made the obvious videos in their subject twice.

A podcast solves that by outsourcing it. The guest is the format. They arrive carrying their own stories, their own material, and their own opinions, and the host's job shrinks to being interested for two hours. You are no longer required to have an idea. You're required to have a chair.

And the guest brings something else, which is their audience — a cross-introduction, the same mechanism that makes group content work, except you get a new one every week without living with anybody.

Problem three is the one nobody says out loud, and it's the heaviest.

Being alone on camera is an enormous psychological load. Every solo creator is the single point of contact between an audience and a personality, and that audience wants more of the person than any person actually has. There's nowhere to hide, and no one to hand a bad day to.

A co-host changes the physics of that completely. The attachment is to the dynamic rather than to you individually. You can be flat for an episode and the show still works, because there's someone else in the room. Two hours of conversation is dramatically less exposing than twenty minutes of talking directly into a lens, even though it's six times the footage.

That's the same move a group makes, arrived at by someone who doesn't have a group. A podcast is the smallest possible version of the thing that keeps collectives alive: another person to carry it when you can't.

Problem four is arithmetic, and it's the one that made the whole thing look irresistible.

Watch time compounds and view count doesn't. Two hours of moderately engaging conversation produces more total watched minutes than ten minutes of brilliance, and the systems that decide who gets shown to whom care a great deal about that.

And podcasts have a second property that almost nothing else in online video has: people search for them. Not for the show — for the guest. Someone types a name into a box, and your two-hour episode from eleven months ago is the result. That's pull discovery, in a medium that otherwise runs almost entirely on push, which means podcast archives age far better than ordinary video archives.

So: cheap, self-generating, psychologically survivable, and it accumulates. Four problems, one room.

At which point the obvious question is why the podcast boom didn't make everyone rich.

And the answer is embarrassing in its simplicity. Everyone read the same four problems.

A low barrier to entry is only an advantage while it's yours. The same two chairs are available to everyone else with the same complaint, and there were a lot of them. Supply exploded — not gradually, but in the space of about two years, across every niche simultaneously.

That alone would be survivable. The thing that makes it brutal is on the demand side, and it's the part almost nobody accounts for before starting.

Podcast consumption is time-bound in a way that short content simply isn't.

A person can watch fifty short videos in a day without noticing. They cannot listen to fifty podcasts. Long-form audio gets consumed in specific, fixed slots — the commute, the gym, the walk, the washing up — and there are only so many of those in a week. It's a finite inventory of hours.

And that inventory is already allocated. To shows people have listened to for years, out of habit, in a slot that belongs to that show. You're not competing for attention in a feed. You're asking someone to evict an incumbent from their Tuesday morning.

Which explains the very specific way new podcasts fail.

They don't fail by being bad. Most of them are perfectly decent — two capable people having a reasonable conversation, competently recorded.

They fail by being *fine*. Because nobody abandons a show they already like for a show that is also fine. The bar isn't quality in the abstract; it's being enough better than an existing habit to justify breaking it. That is a far higher bar, and it's invisible to anyone judging their own show on whether the episodes are good.

Habit is the moat. Incumbency in this format is unusually strong, and it compounds every week a listener doesn't leave.

And there's a sponsorship consequence to that which quietly decides who survives.

Podcast advertising is unusually valuable, because a host reading a message to a habitual listener converts far better than a banner ever will. That's the whole appeal of the format to advertisers, and it's why podcast sponsorship rates can look absurd next to the audience size.

But it's priced on the habit, not the download. A show with a small, weekly, loyal audience is worth more per listener than a large show people dip into occasionally — and advertisers, who track this properly, know exactly which one they're buying.

So the new show is competing for money on the one dimension it definitionally cannot have yet. You can produce a great first season. You cannot produce three years of somebody's Tuesday.

Which is why so many podcasts die at the point where the hosts think they're succeeding. The episodes are good, the numbers are respectable, the feedback is warm — and the money doesn't arrive, because the thing being sold was never the episode.


So what does clear that bar?

Only about three things, in practice. Access — guests that nobody else can get, which turns each episode into an event rather than an instalment. Specificity — a subject narrow enough that you own it outright, where being the only show about the thing beats being the ninth best show about everything. Or genuine chemistry, the kind where two people are funnier together than either is alone.

And that last one deserves an honest note, because it's the one everyone bets on: chemistry cannot be planned. Two people who get on well in private are frequently dull on a microphone, and pairs who barely knew each other sometimes have it immediately. You find out after you've committed, in public, which is the same trap the content houses walked into with a bigger budget.

So here's the thing worth taking from all of it.

The podcast wasn't a format that everyone happened to like. It was a shelter — a rational retreat from an escalating production war, an idea drought, an exposure problem, and a metric that rewards duration.

Retreating was the correct call. It just stops being a retreat the moment everybody else arrives.

And then it's not shelter at all.

It's a crowded room, with better chairs.

## Before you record

No download numbers, no listener statistics, no sponsorship rates on screen unless
you source them. The pattern claims here — flat cost curve, habit-priced ad rates,
strong incumbency, finite listening slots — are all sound as reasoning and none of
them are measured figures in this script. Keep them as reasoning and the video is
solid.

Don't name podcasts that failed. Naming the successful ones as examples of access
or specificity is fine and useful; naming a show as an example of "fine" is a
gratuitous swipe at people doing decent work.

The four-problems framework is the argument. Present it as yours, because a viewer
who disagrees with one of the four still has three, and that's a good comment
section rather than a bad one.

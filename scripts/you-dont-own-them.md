# You Don't Own Them

**Topic:** Platform dependency — what the subscribe button actually does
**Angle:** Creators think they own an audience. They own a permission that a third party can throttle, reprice, or withdraw, with no notice and no appeal. Everyone is a tenant.
**Audience:** Anyone who has ever said "I have 400,000 subscribers" as though it were a possession.
**Tone:** Calm and slightly clinical. The facts are alarming enough without help.
**Open on:** A video that reaches a fraction of the people who asked for it.
**Close on:** Nobody gets evicted while things are going well.

---

## Script

A creator with a million subscribers uploads a video.

It reaches a fraction of them. Not a tenth of a percent, not a catastrophe — just a slice. And that isn't a punishment, or a shadowban, or a conspiracy. That's a normal Tuesday, and it's how the system is supposed to work.

So it's worth asking what the subscribe button actually did.

Because a million people took a deliberate action to say: show me this person's videos. And the system's response to that instruction is, roughly, we'll take it under advisement.

The button is not a subscription. It's a preference signal. A hint passed to a recommendation engine, weighted against several hundred other hints, most of which are about what keeps you on the platform generally rather than what you asked for specifically.

Which means you don't have a distribution channel. You have a suggestion box.

And once that lands, the rest of the picture rearranges itself fairly quickly.

Because if the recommender decides who sees your work, then your relationship with your audience is mediated — permanently, on every single upload — by a third party whose interests overlap with yours but do not match them.

The platform's job is to maximise time spent on the platform. Not time spent on you. Those two goals agree beautifully right up until the moment they don't, and when they disagree, it isn't close. If someone else's video keeps a viewer around longer than yours would, that viewer gets the other video. Nobody is being punished. The machine is doing its actual job.

You are, in the most literal sense, a supplier. And suppliers get substituted.

Which is a workable arrangement, in fairness. Plenty of businesses run on someone else's distribution. It only becomes dangerous because of the second thing, which is that the terms can change and you are not party to the negotiation.

Monetisation rules change. Categories get reclassified. A format gets promoted so hard that everyone must adopt it or shrink — and the effort you'd built up in the old format doesn't transfer. Rules get applied retroactively to work you made years earlier under different guidance.

None of this involves malice. It involves a company adjusting its product, which it is entitled to do, and several hundred thousand small businesses discovering simultaneously that their revenue was a policy setting.

There's no negotiation. There's rarely an appeal that a human being reads. And there is no notice period, because you were never an employee, a partner, or a client. You were a user, and you clicked agree.

And if this sounds like a new problem, it very much isn't. It's the oldest pattern on the internet, and it has run at least three times already.

Publishers built enormous businesses on social feed traffic, hired against it, and then watched organic reach for pages decline steadily until the traffic that funded the newsroom was gone. Developers built companies inside app stores and discovered the terms, the cut, and the rules belonged to someone else. Sellers built brands on marketplaces that could and did launch competing own-brand versions of their best products.

Every one of those groups made the same category error, and it's a subtle one. They confused *access* to a market with *ownership* of a market. Access is enormously valuable. It is also lent to you.

The pattern is so consistent it's almost a law: when a platform is growing, it is generous to suppliers, because it needs supply. When it matures, it stops needing you specifically, and the generosity is reallocated to whatever it needs next.

Nothing about video is exempt from that. It's just earlier in the cycle.


But the ban isn't the real risk, and this is the part people miss.

Getting removed is loud. It's visible, it's appealable in public, other creators rally, journalists occasionally write it up. It's the worst outcome and also the most survivable, because everyone can see it happened.

The dangerous version is quiet. Not removal. Just less.

There is no notification that reads "we have decided to show you to fewer people." The graph simply bends. And from the inside, that is completely indistinguishable from your work getting worse.

So the creator does what any conscientious person does. Assumes it's them. Changes the thumbnails. Changes the format. Changes the length, the pacing, the intro, chases whatever the current advice is — and every change resets whatever consistency they had, which makes the graph worse, which confirms the diagnosis.

An enormous amount of creator burnout is people taking personal responsibility for a parameter change. There's no way to tell the difference, so they assume the version that's their fault.

And out of that opacity comes something genuinely odd: an entire folk religion.

Listen to how creators talk about the recommender and you'll hear a personality. It likes this. It punishes that. It's been weird lately. It hates me. Everyone knows it's software. Everyone talks about it as though it has moods, because the feedback you get is delayed, noisy, and never explained, which is precisely the condition under which humans start inventing causes.

Post at this exact time. Never edit the title in the first hour. Always edit the title in the first hour. Some of these are real. Some are one person's coincidence, repeated confidently, spreading through an advice industry that has no way to test anything and every incentive to sound certain.

The honest position is that nobody outside the building knows how the ranking works, including the people selling courses about it, and probably including a fair number of people inside the building.

Which is a strange foundation for a career — but it's the foundation, so the sensible response isn't better superstition. It's needing the machine less.


Which raises the only question that matters here: what can you actually own?

Not much, and the list is short enough to say out loud.

An email address, or a phone number — a direct line that works if the platform vanishes tonight. A product, which is a relationship with a customer rather than a viewer. And a name people search for.

That last one is badly underrated. There's a real difference between an audience that found you because a machine served you to them, and an audience that types your name into a box. The first is traffic. The second is closer to demand, and demand travels. If your viewers can only find you by being handed to you, you don't have an audience, you have a placement.

The test is blunt and worth running honestly: if the platform disappeared overnight, what could you take with you? Whatever survives that question is the actual business. Everything else is occupancy.

Except there's a catch on the escape route too, and it's the reason most attempts fail.

Everyone has heard "build an email list." Most creators who build one end up with a list nobody opens, and conclude email is dead. It isn't. The problem is that attention doesn't port automatically.

The audience formed a habit inside a specific format, on a specific platform, at a specific moment in their day. None of that comes with them. Moving people requires giving them a reason to move that's as good as the reason they came in the first place — something that only exists over there, and that they'd actually miss.

"Sign up for updates" is not a reason. It's an admin request addressed to someone who isn't your employee.

And it's why the genuine diversification move is so often misunderstood. Uploading the same video to four platforms is not diversification. That's four tenancies, four landlords, four sets of terms you don't control, and the same underlying asset — someone else's recommendation engine — in all four.

Real diversification changes the *type* of the thing you own, not the number of places you rent.

So the point of all this isn't that platforms are villains, or that you should leave, or that any of this is avoidable. You can't opt out of distribution. That's where the people are.

The point is to be accurate about what you have.

You have a tenancy. A good one, possibly, on generous terms, with a landlord who is mostly reasonable and often genuinely useful. But the terms are theirs, the rent is collected in advance in the form of your work, and the lease is month to month.

And nobody ever gets evicted while things are going well.

That's exactly the problem. The arrangement is invisible for as long as it's working, which is precisely the entire period during which you could have done something about it.

## Before you record

One factual dependency: the historical precedents. Social publishers losing
organic reach, app-store terms, marketplace own-brands — all three are real and
well documented, but if you name a company or put a percentage on screen, source
it. The safe version is the pattern, stated as a pattern.

Do not name a specific creator's suppression or ban as an example. Every one of
those stories has a disputed version, you will not have the full facts, and the
argument works without it.

The claim that burnout is often a misattributed parameter change is a strong
reading, not a measured finding. Keep it phrased as a reading.

Two things to be careful about in tone. First, don't drift into "the algorithm is
out to get you" — the script's actual claim is duller and stronger: the machine is
indifferent, which is worse. Second, avoid implying anyone is owed distribution.
The argument is about being accurate, not aggrieved.

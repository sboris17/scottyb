# Your Niche Is Your Salary

**Topic:** Why identical channels earn wildly different money
**Angle:** You aren't paid for views. You're paid for the commercial value of the audience you assembled — so choosing a niche is choosing a salary band, years before you find out what it was.
**Audience:** People who assume big channel equals big money and are confused when the numbers leak.
**Tone:** Matter-of-fact. This is arithmetic, not injustice.
**Open on:** Two identical channels, one earning several times the other.
**Close on:** Read the contract before you sign it for five years.

---

## Script

Two channels. Same subscriber count. Same views per video, same upload schedule, same length, same amount of work going in.

One of them earns several times what the other does.

Not from sponsorships. Not from merch. From the same ad system, on the same platform, for the same number of human beings watching the same amount of video.

Neither creator is better than the other. Neither is being punished. And the difference between them was effectively decided before either of them uploaded anything at all.

Here's the sentence that explains it, and it's the one nobody puts on the thumbnail: you are not paid for views.

You are paid for what someone else is willing to pay to reach the specific people who showed up.

Which is a completely different business than the one most people think they're in.

Because there's an auction happening under every video, and you're not in it. Advertisers are bidding — not on your content, not on your production quality, not on how hard you worked. They're bidding on an audience profile. Your video is just the place that audience happens to be standing.

So the question that sets your income isn't "how good is this?" It's "what is a person in this audience worth to a company trying to sell something?"

And once you ask it that way, some very uncomfortable rankings fall straight out.

What makes an audience expensive is boring and mercenary. Purchase intent — is this person close to a decision to buy something. Money — do they have any. Where they live, because ad markets are priced by country. And whether the content around them is safe enough that a large cautious brand will allow its name near it.

Score highly on all four and advertisers fight over you. Score badly and they don't show up, and the rate you get is whatever's left.

That's why a channel about business software, or insurance, or investing, earns money that seems absurd next to its view count. The audience is small, but a meaningful fraction of it is a person actively looking to spend a lot of money on exactly the thing being advertised. One conversion pays for thousands of impressions.

And it's why entertainment — gaming, comedy, reaction, most of the stuff people actually love — sits at the other end. Enormous audiences. Fantastic engagement. Genuine cultural weight. And a viewer who, in that moment, is not in the market for anything at all.

That's not a judgement about the work. Some of the most skilled video being made anywhere is in the cheapest categories to advertise against. It's a judgement about the customer, made by someone who has never watched your channel and never will.

And then there's the fourth factor, which is the one that quietly bends what gets made.

Brand safety. Not legality — safety. A large, cautious company with a legal department does not want its logo appearing next to swearing, or violence, or a tragedy, or a war, or a political argument, or in some cases a serious discussion of anyone's health. None of that is against the rules. All of it is a discount.

Follow that through and you get a genuinely strange incentive. Cover something that matters, carefully and responsibly, and you will usually earn less than if you'd covered nothing in particular. Make the same video about the same event with the difficult words removed and the rate improves.

Nobody sends a memo. There's no editorial line, no instruction, nobody telling anyone what to say. There's just a persistent financial breeze blowing in one direction, and after a few years of standing in it, you end up leaning.

Which is worth knowing about even if you decide to ignore it — and plenty of people do, and fund it another way, which we'll come back to.


And then there's geography, which is the one that actually stings.

The same video. The same length, the same watch time, the same viewer behaviour in every measurable respect. It earns differently depending on which countries those viewers are sitting in, because ad spending per head is wildly uneven across the world.

So two creators can do identical work, at identical quality, to identical audiences by size, and one of them is running a real business while the other is running a hobby with a large following — for reasons that have nothing to do with either of them.

Nobody designed that as a punishment. It's just what happens when your income is downstream of somebody else's advertising budget.

And because it's downstream of a budget, it moves like a budget.

Ad rates are seasonal. Companies spend hard in the run-up to Christmas and then, in January, stop — the money is gone, the new year's budget hasn't opened, and rates fall off a cliff. Same channel, same audience, same videos, half the income, for reasons that happened in a meeting you weren't in.

Which means creator income isn't merely low or high. It's volatile in a way salaried people never experience. Your best month and your worst month can be a factor apart with no change in your behaviour at all, and if you plan your life around the good one, the quiet one arrives anyway.


At which point the obvious move looks obvious. Go where the money is. Find the expensive niche, point the camera at it, collect.

And this is where a lot of people set fire to two years.

Because the high-value niches are competitive for exactly the reason you found them — everyone can read the same table. You are not arriving with a secret. You're arriving last, into the most contested space, against people who've been there for years and often actually work in the field.

And the deeper problem is that you have to keep doing it. Not for a month. For years, at volume, on camera, while staying interesting. Faking curiosity about commercial insurance for three years is not a strategy, it's a slow injury, and audiences are unnervingly good at detecting someone who doesn't care. They won't articulate it. They'll just drift.

So the answer isn't to chase the rate. It's to ask a better question than "what pays most."

The question is: where does something I can genuinely sustain overlap with an audience somebody wants to reach?

That intersection is almost always bigger than people assume, because most creators never look for it. They pick a topic based purely on enthusiasm, discover the economics eighteen months later, and conclude the platform is broken.

There's usually an adjacent version of what you love that sits in a better neighbourhood. Not a different passion — a different framing of the same one. The gaming channel that's really about the industry. The fitness channel that's really about the products. The travel channel that's really about the logistics and the money. Same enthusiasm, same person, materially different audience arriving.

And then there's the second lever, which is the one that actually rescues everyone in the cheap categories.

If the ad rate is bad, stop selling ads.

This is why the pattern is so consistent once you look for it. Creators with low-value audiences by advertising standards build brands, launch products, sell merch that people genuinely want, start communities, make things. Creators with high-value audiences frequently do none of that, because they don't have to — the ads alone are enough.

Which means a large, devoted, commercially "worthless" audience isn't worthless at all. It's just worthless *to advertisers*. Sell that audience something they actually want, directly, and you capture the whole value instead of the sliver an ad network passes down. That's the entire logic of every creator-owned product line that ever worked.

The advertising rate is not a verdict on your audience. It's one buyer's opinion, and you are not obliged to accept it as the only offer.

It also reopens the brand-safety question, because a discount only has power over you while ads are the whole income. Creators who sell directly to their own audience can cover the difficult subject, keep the difficult word in, and take the hit on a revenue line that was never the main one. The people who can afford to be uncompromising are almost always the people who got paid somewhere else first.


So the real lesson here has nothing to do with rate cards.

Your niche is not a topic. It's the terms of your employment. It sets what you get paid per unit of attention, who your actual customer is, how competitive your market is, and which revenue models are even available to you.

Most people choose it in an afternoon, based on what they felt like talking about, and then live inside that decision for five years wondering why the effort isn't converting.

Choose it like it's a contract.

Because it is one.

## Before you record

**Do not put a single RPM or CPM number on screen unless you can source it.**
The general shape here — finance and software at the top, entertainment and gaming
at the bottom, large gaps between countries, a Q4 peak and a January collapse — is
well established and safe to state qualitatively. The specific figures move
constantly, differ by channel, and are the thing viewers in this niche will check
first.

If you want numbers, the honest options are your own analytics with a screenshot,
or a creator who has publicly published theirs, quoted and dated. Anything else is
a guess wearing a decimal point.

Two framings to keep as framings, not facts: that brand safety produces an
editorial drift, and that adjacent niches let you keep the passion and change the
economics. Both are strong arguments. Neither is a measured effect.
